## Patterns supported across the sample

1. **Question first, explanation second.** Titles frame a curiosity gap around a familiar behavior, danger or ancient-human practice. The first visuals quickly instantiate the question with a person, prop, animal or explicit contradiction.
2. **One central puzzle carries the explanation.** The samples are arranged around answering their title question, rather than presenting a disconnected list of facts.
3. **Move between story and evidence.** Characters/objects make a situation legible; maps, time lines, charts, labels and split-screen comparisons explain causes or change across time.
4. **Make abstract comparisons visible.** Past versus present, environment versus body, and danger versus attraction appear as labeled or side-by-side images.
5. **Use text as an image element.** Hand-lettered labels and larger headings appear in several long-form storyboards. The target Short adds a persistent lower spoken-caption layer. The exact color, font and placement vary by piece and should not be treated as a universal rule.
6. **Visual treatment changes with subject and mood.** The samples include bright flat diagrams, parchment-like historical drawings, warm landscape scenes and dark night illustrations. The recurring device is narration-driven illustration and explanation, not one fixed palette or material texture.

These observations support a flexible sequence for an original 45–60 second video: (a) concrete question, (b) correct or complicate the obvious answer, (c) show the starting conditions, (d) explain two or three causal steps, (e) compare outcomes over time or place, (f) return to the opening image with a precise payoff. The number and duration of shots should follow the narration; the sample does not establish a mandatory shot quota.

## Adaptation guidance

Transfer the channel's **functions**—curiosity-led opening, fast premise clarification, visual analogies, maps/timelines and a synthesis image—to a new subject. Write new narration and evidence, use new scene solutions and select visuals that serve the new explanation. Do not adopt the video's exact “black to white” phrasing, use its script or figures, or treat its character shapes, jokes, voice, subtitle color or artwork as assets to reproduce.

For Video Pilot work, preserve the current brief, cast and project constraints. A reference analysis does not change the project's channel profile, language, voice, prompt templates, provider, aspect ratio or production mode.
