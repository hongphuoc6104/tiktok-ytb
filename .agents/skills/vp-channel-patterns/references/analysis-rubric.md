# Reference-video analysis rubric

Use this when a user wants to understand how a creator's videos work. The goal is to explain decisions another creator can apply to a different subject, not to recreate the source.

## Resolve the source and sample set

1. Open the supplied video through public, ordinary access. Record its title, creator, channel ID, URL, duration, format/aspect when known, and view count with the retrieval date.
2. Use the channel ID to avoid mixing similarly named channels. Inspect the requested format tab and list the available examples before choosing the comparison set.
3. When asked for a top five, state whether the target is included. Prefer the five highest-view relevant videos other than the target when the target is already being analyzed separately. If the channel has fewer than five relevant Shorts, add its highest-view comparable videos in other formats and clearly label them as long-form or otherwise different.
4. Prefer the platform's public metadata for titles, durations and views. Counts are snapshots, not lifetime guarantees. Do not present search snippets or a similarly named channel as channel-owned evidence.

## Gather evidence without overclaiming

Use the highest-quality public evidence available: the video itself, an authorized local copy provided by the user, accessible captions/transcript, player metadata, and public storyboard/contact-sheet frames. A user request to analyze a link does not require account login, paid APIs, fresh browser profiles, private endpoints, or bypassing access controls. If direct media is not ordinarily available, continue with captions and public storyboard samples and document the missing audio/video evidence.

For each source, note what was actually inspected:

- **Video/audio inspected:** frame-by-frame cut and effect analysis, caption timing, speech, pauses, music and sound effects can be described within the inspected portion.
- **Transcript only:** words and timing can be analyzed, but visual design, effects and audio performance remain unknown.
- **Storyboard samples only:** describe visible compositions and their sampled timestamps. Do not infer exact cuts, motion, layer count, animation, music, effects or silence from still frames.
- **Metadata only:** describe only title, public counts, duration and available format metadata.

Keep confidence tied to evidence. Mark an observation as direct, an inference, or unverified. Cite the source video beside each source-specific finding.

## Measure the target

Use a time-coded table with a practical granularity for the format. For each interval, record:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Spoken idea | A concise paraphrase of the narration or the best-supported idea; do not reconstruct missing lines from a title. |
| Story beat | Hook, question, setup, explanation, contrast, complication, payoff or loop. |
| Visual function | Character action, prop, diagram, map, timeline, comparison, title card or empty hold. |
| On-screen text | Spoken captions versus labels, dialogue balloons and graphic headlines; describe position, line length, color, backing and readability. |
| Edit/pacing | Observed shot changes, hold length, transition, zoom/pan or speed only when motion is available. |
| Sound | Narration style, music, effects, transitions and silence only when audio was listened to. |

Use these terms consistently:

- A **story beat** is a change in the argument or narrative function.
- A **shot/composition** is a continuous visual setup. A redraw, crop or camera move may or may not be a new shot; state the counting rule.
- An **image asset** is a unique source illustration or layer. Still samples cannot establish the exact asset count.
- A **storyboard sample** is one frame captured at a stated interval. It is neither a cut nor a source asset.

When only storyboard samples are available, give a lower-bound or range for distinct compositions and list the sample interval. Explain that faster cuts or hidden reuse may change the count.

## Compare the channel

For each selected video, capture the same small set of observations: opening question/promise, opening visual, sequence of explanatory devices, use of labels/captions, transitions between scenario and explanation, payoff, ending, and inspected format. Compare at least three videos before calling a trait a recurring convention. Separate:

- **Repeatable conventions:** patterns visible in several samples, such as question-led titles, concrete scenarios, recurring use of diagrams or contrast.
- **Episode-specific choices:** color palette, jokes, props, character poses, subject-specific graphics or one-off effects.
- **Performance unknowns:** watch-time/retention, audience intent, exact music choice or source assets not visible in public material.

Do not claim that a pattern caused views. A view count measures exposure, not why a viewer watched or stayed.

## Report shape

Use this order unless the user requests another format:

1. Scope, retrieval date and evidence level.
2. Target metadata and time-coded breakdown.
3. Target estimates: beats, compositions/shots, sample count and caption/audio findings, with counting rules.
4. Comparison set: selection basis, current view counts, durations, and per-video observations.
5. Cross-video commonalities and important variations.
6. Original production playbook, confidence notes and unresolved questions.

Keep copyrighted speech in paraphrase. A few short phrases may illustrate wording, but do not substitute a copied transcript for analysis.
