# Turning analysis into an original production playbook

Read this after completing the evidence table. Use patterns that recur across several reference videos; do not elevate one video's unusual choice into a channel rule.

## Build the playbook from functions

Describe what a choice does rather than copying how the reference draws or phrases it. For example:

- “Open on a recognizable problem, then reveal why the obvious explanation is incomplete” is transferable.
- A creator's exact joke, character design, line, title-card wording, voice, music cue or scene order is not a reusable template.
- A diagram is a function. The reference's exact diagram, labels and arrangement are source-specific.

For each recurring pattern, record: evidence across the sample, the audience question it serves, the production decision it suggests, one way it could fit a different subject, and one risk to avoid.

## Draft a flexible short-form structure

Use a timing range, not a fixed shot quota. A 45–60 second explainer may use this as a starting point, then change it to fit the subject and narration:

| Portion | Narrative job | Possible visual job |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Ask a clear question or show a surprising consequence. | One readable action, object or contrast. |
| Reframe | Correct an oversimplification or establish the real puzzle. | A reveal, crossed-out assumption, or comparison. |
| Explanation | Give two or three causal steps, each with one idea. | Use a map, timeline, labeled process or concrete analogy only where it reduces effort. |
| Synthesis | Connect the steps and show what changed. | Before/after, gradient, scale or a return to the opening image. |
| Ending | Deliver a specific payoff or a clean loop to the opening question. | Hold long enough to read the final point. |

Do not force every section when the narration or target duration calls for a different structure. Build the narration first, then assign a visual purpose to each beat; do not add images just to satisfy a fixed count.

## Specify captions and sound as separate tracks

Treat spoken captions, burned-in subtitles, dialogue balloons, labels and headline cards as separate elements. In the new production brief, state:

- whether captions are burned in, platform captions, or both;
- maximum words and lines on screen at once;
- placement inside the safe area, contrast/backing and emphasis behavior;
- how long each phrase stays readable and whether graphic labels may overlap it;
- narration, music, effects and intentional quiet as independent timeline cues.

If the reference audio was not available, leave its voice/music/effect choices as unverified questions. Do not invent a voice match from captions or imitate a named creator's voice.

## Adapt to Video Pilot

The reference playbook supplies evidence about storytelling and editing choices; it does not replace a job's brief or the channel profile. For a Video Pilot production request:

1. Keep the user's subject and learning/content goal separate from the style reference.
2. Translate the selected playbook into a brief: audience, one-sentence promise, narration beats, each beat's visual purpose, readable on-screen text, pacing, caption behavior and audio needs.
3. Keep the project's approved cast, prompt templates, provider routing, voice choices, language, aspect ratio and workflow mode intact unless the user separately authorized a change.
4. Route actual creation, continuation or repair through `vp-production`; this analysis skill does not itself grant production scope or approve external submissions.
5. Review the resulting video against its own brief. Similarity means that the explanatory device works, not that it reproduces the reference's distinctive expression.

## Final adaptation check

Before handing off a playbook, confirm that it:

- names the source videos and labels measured versus inferred claims;
- describes recurring mechanics supported by multiple examples;
- offers a flexible structure with adjustable timing and scene count;
- uses a different subject, examples, script, visual assets and scene solutions;
- preserves existing Video Pilot policies and leaves audio unknown where it was not heard;
- gives the next producer enough information to act without copying the source.
